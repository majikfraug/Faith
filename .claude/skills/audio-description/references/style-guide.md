# Audio description style guide

Based on common practice in the Described and Captioned Media Program (DCMP) Description Key, the American
Council of the Blind's Audio Description Project guidelines, and streaming-service style guides, adapted to a
finished documentary-style film with foreign-language dialogue and subtitles.

## Purpose

A listener who cannot see the picture should get the same story, in the same order, with the same emphasis,
as a sighted viewer, and should never be told what the sighted viewer is not told yet.

## Priorities when time is short

1. On-screen text that carries meaning: titles, names, places, dates, scripture references, captions.
2. Who is present and who is speaking, when the voice alone does not tell you.
3. Where and when: scene changes, location, time of day, passage of time.
4. The action that moves the story: what someone does, hands over, picks up, walks toward.
5. Reactions and expressions that a sighted viewer would read: a nod, tears, a smile, a look away.
6. Appearance and setting: clothing, age, surroundings, weather, colour. Only when time allows.

Cut from the bottom of this list first.

## Language

- Present tense, third person: “Romulo opens the book.”
- Active, concrete verbs and nouns. Avoid “there is”, “we see”, “the camera pans”, “the scene shows”.
- Objective: describe what is visible, not what it means. “He lowers his head” rather than “he is sad”;
  “she is crying” is fine because tears are visible.
- Specific over general: “a wooden church with a tin roof” beats “a building”.
- One idea per line. Short sentences. A fragment is fine when it is clear: “Night. A dirt road.”
- Consistent vocabulary for recurring people and places. Keep a glossary.
- Neutral tone; the narrator is a quiet guide, not a commentator. No judgments, no jokes, no explanations of symbolism.
- Don't describe sounds the listener can hear (a knock, applause, a car) unless the source is unclear.
- Avoid technical film words (close-up, cut to, montage) unless the technique itself matters to the story.

## Identifying people

- Until the film names someone, describe them with one or two stable traits (“a man in a blue shirt”, “an older woman”).
- The first time the name is known (spoken, captioned, or in a subtitle), link it once: “The man in the blue shirt, Romulo, ...”
- After that use the name. Never use a name the audience has not been given yet.
- When several voices alternate and the listener could lose track, say who speaks in the previous gap
  (“Romulo and his wife Donna talk at the table”) or prefix a subtitle read with the name.

## Timing

- Only in gaps of speech. Over music, ambience and sound effects is fine.
- Describe before or with the visual, never after it has passed, and never ahead of a reveal.
- Leave a breath at both ends of a gap (the tools reserve 0.2 s in and 0.3 s out).
- Short windows get short lines. If a window is under about 1.5 seconds, usually say nothing unless one word matters (“Night.”).
- Long quiet stretches do not need to be filled. Silence lets the listener take in the music and the moment.
- A scene change deserves a line even when little happens: new place, new time, who is there.

## On-screen text

- Read verbatim, in reading order, with a short tag so the listener knows it is text: “Title:”, “Caption:”, “Text on screen:”.
- Scripture references and quotations: read exactly as written, including the reference (“John 3, verse 16”).
- Names and roles in lower thirds: “Caption: Romulo Sauñe, pastor.”
- Credits: read the main credits that matter (title, the organisation, dedication); summarize long rolls (“Credits roll.”).
- Foreign-language text with an on-screen translation: read the translation; say the language if it matters.

## Subtitle reads

- The listener hears the original voice start, then the English read over it (the tools delay subtitle reads
  by 0.3 s and duck the original 14 dB). Read clearly and neutrally; do not act, do not imitate accents.
- Keep the subtitle's meaning, tense and register. Condense only when the read cannot fit; cut repetition and filler first.
- Where one subtitle contains two speakers (“– Who is it? – It's me.”), read both; add names only if the listener could not tell.
- Lyrics and prayers: if the words are shown and carry meaning, read them; otherwise summarize.

## Things to avoid

- Interpretation, intention, and emotion that is not visible.
- Describing over dialogue, or describing what dialogue already says.
- Spoilers: do not name a character, place or event before the film reveals it.
- Filler words, “beautiful”, “interesting”, and evaluative adjectives.
- Reading cue numbers or timecodes aloud.
- Changing the film's meaning: when a scene is ambiguous on purpose, keep it ambiguous.

## Checklist before approving a range

- Every line sits inside a speech gap (fit = yes, or tight with a reason).
- Every on-screen text that matters has a TEXT cue.
- Names are introduced before they are used; the glossary agrees with the lines.
- Present tense throughout; no “we see”; no interpretation.
- Nothing duplicates what the audio already tells the listener.
- The scene changes are marked.
- Read the lines aloud in order: do they tell the story in the right order, with the right emphasis?
